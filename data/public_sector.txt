=== Site 11: INESdata incubator ===
The INESData Legal Data Space represents one of the most ambitious attempts in Spain and in the broader European landscape to structure, share, and enhance the value of legal data through modern data-space technologies. Its purpose is to create a trusted ecosystem in which legal information can circulate securely, responsibly, and efficiently across different organizations that traditionally operate in silos. Legal data is, by its nature, sensitive, fragmented, and often difficult to access or reuse, and yet it constitutes an enormous source of value for public administrations, private companies, universities, researchers, and legal-tech innovators. INESData seeks to unlock this value by building an infrastructure capable of ensuring data sovereignty, interoperability, and compliance, while enabling advanced AI services that can analyze, classify, and extract insights from complex legal texts. The legal data space is one of the four verticals developed within INESData, alongside linguistic, mobility, and media data spaces. Its creation follows the architecture and principles promoted by major European initiatives such as Gaia-X and IDSA, which define how trustworthy, federated, and sovereign data infrastructures should operate. In this context, the legal data space becomes not merely a repository of documents but a living ecosystem where participants retain full control over their data while making it usable under specific, negotiated conditions. This federated model ensures that data providers, whether they are public institutions or private entities, do not lose ownership or control; instead, they can expose metadata, define access policies, and enable data consumers to request, negotiate, and use the information in a controlled manner. The technological backbone of the legal data space is built around the Dataspace Connector, based on the Eclipse Dataspace Connector architecture, which guarantees secure and policy-enforced data exchanges between participants. This connector allows organizations to publish what data they make available, under which terms, and for which uses, ensuring transparency and trust in the exchange process. Additionally, semantic interoperability plays a crucial role: because legal terminology is complex and often domain-specific, INESData integrates vocabulary management tools and ontologies that help structure and harmonize the description of legal datasets. This avoids misunderstandings and ensures that different actors interpret shared information consistently. On top of this infrastructure, INESData provides a layer of artificial intelligence services designed to extract value from legal documents. These include natural language processing systems capable of identifying clauses, recognizing entities, summarizing texts, or classifying regulatory information, as well as knowledge-graph tools that represent legal entities, relationships, and obligations in a structured way. Together, these tools enable much deeper and more efficient analysis of legal data than traditional manual methods, opening the door to legal analytics applications, contract intelligence, automated compliance checks, and research on large-scale legal corpora. A concrete example of how INESData applies these technologies is the 3CEL corpus, a carefully curated and annotated collection of Spanish public procurement contracts. This dataset includes hundreds of tenders and thousands of labels identifying relevant legal clauses, making it a valuable resource for training machine learning and NLP systems in the legal domain. Such a corpus does not merely illustrate the potential of legal data sharing; it actively enables innovation by providing a benchmark through which models can be developed, evaluated, and improved. It also demonstrates how legal-tech solutions can be built more effectively when data is made available in structured and interoperable formats. Beyond the technical aspects, governance is central to the functioning of the legal data space. INESData has developed a governance handbook that defines roles, responsibilities, contractual models, and operational rules for participants. This includes how data contracts are negotiated, how usage policies are enforced, and how disputes or compliance issues are managed. A clear governance structure is essential in the legal domain, where confidentiality, intellectual property, regulatory constraints, and liability concerns are typically very high. Through this framework, participants can engage confidently, knowing that the ecosystem is designed to protect their interests while promoting collaboration. The impact of such an initiative extends far beyond the technical sphere. For legal professionals, the data space can streamline contract review, accelerate due-diligence processes, provide benchmarks for clause drafting, and reduce the cost of repetitive analysis tasks. Public administrations can benefit from increased transparency, better procurement oversight, and more efficient access to normative and regulatory information. Researchers and universities can gain access to a wealth of legal data that is often inaccessible due to fragmentation or confidentiality constraints, enabling new avenues of academic study. Legal-tech companies can build new tools and services on top of the data space, stimulating innovation and potentially giving rise to a broader ecosystem of applications powered by shared legal knowledge. The legal data space also aligns with the European vision of creating domain-specific data ecosystems that foster cross-border cooperation and strengthen Europe’s digital sovereignty. By adhering to Gaia-X principles and IDSA standards, INESData ensures that its architecture is compatible with emerging European data-space initiatives, enabling potential future integration and expansion. This positions Spain as an active contributor to the evolving European data economy and showcases how public and private actors can collaborate to build infrastructures that are both technologically advanced and ethically grounded. Despite its potential, the project faces challenges. Legal data often contains personal information or sensitive business content, requiring careful anonymization, privacy-preserving mechanisms, and strict access controls. The heterogeneity of legal texts makes semantic alignment difficult, requiring constant updates to vocabularies and ontologies. Governance frameworks must evolve as new participants join and new legal requirements emerge. Adoption requires convincing organizations to integrate connectors, publish data, and trust a shared infrastructure. Ensuring the long-term sustainability of the data space—financially, technically, and institutionally—is also crucial, especially after the initial project phase. Nonetheless, the INESData Legal Data Space stands as a milestone in the digital transformation of the legal sector. It combines cutting-edge data-space technologies, semantic frameworks, AI capabilities, and governance models to create a secure, sovereign, and interoperable environment for legal data sharing. If successfully adopted and expanded, it has the potential to transform how legal information is accessed, analyzed, and leveraged, making legal knowledge more actionable, transparent, and integrated across different segments of society. By fostering collaboration and innovation, it brings the legal domain into the broader movement toward data-driven ecosystems, contributing to a more efficient, intelligent, and interconnected future for legal services and public governance. The project “Infrastructure for the Research of Distributed Data Spaces at UPM” is established as a fundamental pillar in the creation of a national incubator for data spaces and Artificial Intelligence services. With the objective of leveraging the advantages of federated infrastructures in the cloud and at the edge, INESData positions itself as a reference for promoting collaborative research and innovation at national level within the European Data Spaces initiative. Aware of the importance of disseminating knowledge and promoting the project to ensure community engagement, INESData has implemented a comprehensive communication and dissemination strategy. This strategy includes the creation and management of a dedicated website, active participation in relevant events and the production of specialised publications. Likewise, integration into national and international collaboration networks is a key axis to strengthen the project’s impact and sustainability. Together, these initiatives seek to consolidate INESData as a national reference in federated cloud-edge infrastructures and in the research of distributed data spaces, fostering visibility, impact and knowledge exchange in this strategic field. The INESData project website serves as the central hub for all information related to the initiative. In this virtual space, users will find details on objectives, progress, participating researchers and the resources and tools that have been developed. The website functions as an information channel for the community, facilitating access to relevant documents, repositories, news and organised events. The transparency and accessibility provided through the website ensure a direct connection with stakeholders and encourage active participation in the initiative. During this period, the Repositories section has been enabled, offering an overview of the data and source-code repositories used in the development of the infrastructure for researching distributed data spaces. It presents the different repositories implemented, including their specific purposes, contents and access links. To strengthen project visibility and promote collaboration with the community, a news section has been created where the most relevant updates, events and project milestones are shared. This section seeks to keep stakeholders informed and connected with the activities shaping the future of data spaces in Spain. In addition, a contact form has been enabled for those who wish to obtain further information or explore collaboration opportunities with INESData. Whether for researchers, companies or public administrations, this form is the gateway for joining the innovation ecosystem driven by the project. To offer a complete view of the progress and services developed within INESData, specific pages have also been created for each vertical data space and the associated services, which are gradually incorporated into the website. These pages describe the functionalities, objectives and use cases of each initiative, allowing users to explore and actively participate in the INESData ecosystem. The active presence of INESData in national and international events is essential for establishing connections, sharing experiences, and presenting project progress to a diverse audience. Participation in conferences, symposia and workshops provides a space for exchanging ideas, creating strategic alliances for the use of project technologies and services, and taking part in commercial and research projects across the European data-space landscape. Furthermore, the production of specialised publications in scientific journals and conferences contributes to disseminating the knowledge generated by INESData and consolidates its position as a reference in distributed data-spaces research. In this regard, a detailed list of events is included, indicating date, location and INESData team participants, together with links to the event webpages. INESData has actively participated in events and meetings of European associations such as BDVA, Gaia-X and IDSA among others. These encounters have created opportunities to explore new collaborations in order to form consortia for future Horizon Europe proposals and to strengthen its presence in the data-spaces domain. In line with the project objectives, UPM has worked on preparing several 2024 Horizon Europe proposals within topics such as AI-driven data operations and compliance technologies, Explainable and Robust AI, Data Spaces for Cultural Heritage and Fundamentals of Software Engineering. 


=== Site 12: OmicSpace: Federated Platform for Multimodal Data- OmicSpace ===
OmicSpace is a secure and ethical platform for managing health-related data for research and personalized medicine. It fosters institutional collaboration and enables the secure integration of clinical, biobank, and omics data. Founded on trust, security, interoperability, and respect for participant data sovereignty, OmicSpace offers a comprehensive solution to today’s challenges in health data management. Through secure data federation, advanced analytics, and collaboration among key stakeholders, it advances more precise, personalized, and efficient medicine. Supported by the Ministry of Health of the Generalitat Valenciana, the initiative aims to position Spain as a leader in health data governance, promoting the ethical and secure exchange of information in accordance with the FAIR principles — Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable. OmicSpace transforms biomedical information into actionable knowledge to advance personalized medicine. OmicSpace is a federated data space that securely integrates clinical, biobank, and omics data. It drives personalized medicine by improving diagnostics and tailoring treatments to individual patients, converting complex biomedical data into actionable clinical insights to enhance healthcare delivery. The project is led by the Health Research Institute La Fe and the Research Foundation of the Hospital Polytechnic La Fe of the Valencian Community, with the support of the Valencian Ministry of Health. OmicSpace provides secure, cross-border exchange of federated clinical, biobank, and omics data. It ensures interoperability through harmonized standards and technologies, offers high-quality validated data for clinical and research use, and guarantees ethical, privacy-preserving data access compliant with GDPR and privacy-by-design principles. It fosters the development of innovative therapies through advanced analytics and accelerates personalized medicine and scientific discovery, enabling responsible reuse of clinical data and collaboration across healthcare, research, and technology sectors. Its main objective is to integrate and facilitate access to omics data — genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics — alongside phenotypic and clinical information, to advance precision medicine and develop innovative healthcare solutions. OmicSpace addresses critical needs in the health data ecosystem, accelerating research and innovation in personalized medicine. OmicSpace provides a secure data environment that connects the healthcare ecosystem to transform data into knowledge. Its federated data network consists of eight strategic nodes hosted by leading institutions, offering access to high-quality data for research. The platform spans seven Spanish autonomous communities and integrates with the European health data infrastructure. Its datalake architecture consolidates clinical, epidemiological, and genomic data for analysis and integration, supported by advanced digital infrastructure and a renowned research team. OmicSpace relies on modular and interoperable architecture for scalable integration of clinical and omics data. It uses open standards such as OMOP and HL7, and ensures privacy and data sovereignty through OAuth2 and OpenID protocols. It aligns with European regulations, including the European Data Strategy, the Data Act, and the Data Governance Regulation, and complies with IDSA (International Data Spaces Association) standards to ensure global interoperability. OmicSpace is a common data space where research centers, hospitals, public administrations, and private entities securely and voluntarily share and reuse data. It delivers multiple benefits: – For society: data-driven decision-making in healthcare, improved quality of life, and equitable access to services. – For citizens: personalized diagnostics and therapies, access to innovative treatments, and safer healthcare delivery. – For research: high-quality integrated data for biomedical studies, fostering collaboration and visibility. – For professionals: easy access to relevant information, evidence-based recommendations, and continuous professional development. – For industry: access to interoperable, high-value data for product development and efficient clinical trials. OmicSpace drives innovation in Spain by promoting data federation, advanced analytics, and stakeholder collaboration. It is a strategic initiative positioning Spain as a leader in advanced health data management under the coordination of IIS La Fe and the Valencian Health Ministry. OmicSpace enables secure integration and exchange of clinical, genomic, phenotypic, and epidemiological data. It provides a robust regulatory and technical framework for research in personalized medicine, supports predictive model development, and promotes interoperability through open standards aligned with Gaia-X and FAIR data frameworks. It facilitates access to large volumes of high-quality data through a scalable, secure infrastructure, and promotes collaboration between public and private entities while ensuring full compliance with data protection regulations such as the GDPR. It optimizes the reuse of health data, contributing to the advancement of 5P medicine — predictive, preventive, personalized, participatory, and precise. OmicSpace integrates artificial intelligence and data protection through federated learning and compute-to-data strategies. Each institution trains a local model using its own data and shares only model updates with a central server that aggregates them into a global model. This process ensures privacy by design and allows AI to improve healthcare without transferring sensitive data. The OmicSpace architecture includes fifteen layers: data sources (hospitals, labs, biobanks), data ingestion and processing (ETL/ELT), secure lakehouse storage, governance and auditing, data catalogues and publication, ML Ops, federated training, descriptive and predictive analytics, BI reporting, regulated data marketplaces, and analytical consumption governance. It provides end-to-end infrastructure for the entire data lifecycle, ensuring security, privacy, and traceability. A key component is the secondary use of health data within the European Health Data Space (EHDS). Secondary use refers to the reuse of health data generated during medical care — electronic records, genomic data, biobank information, or registries — for research, innovation, and policy development, always under strict privacy and security guarantees. Data are processed in secure environments managed by authorized organizations, anonymized to prevent identification, and accessible only to approved researchers, health authorities, and innovators working on legitimate public-interest projects. OmicSpace applies strong data protection measures including anonymization, pseudonymization, and encryption. It defines strict privacy and access policies, ensuring that only verified users can access sensitive data under predefined conditions. Encryption protocols such as TLS and SSL are implemented for secure communications, while privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs) and continuous vulnerability management ensure resilience. OmicSpace is fully GDPR-compliant and conducts regular security assessments and privacy impact analyses. Through this security-first approach, OmicSpace safeguards citizen privacy and ensures full regulatory compliance. It represents a trusted model for data sharing, governance, and innovation in healthcare, transforming health data into actionable knowledge that drives personalized medicine, predictive analytics, and ethical innovation across Europe.


=== Site 13: Data Finland ===
DataFinland is an initiative created to address a fundamental challenge that has long affected the usability of open data: the lack of rich, consistent, and interoperable metadata. In many countries, large quantities of public data are increasingly being released to promote transparency, innovation, and research. However, when datasets are poorly described, scattered across different portals, or use incompatible vocabularies and metadata standards, they become difficult to discover, understand, and reuse. DataFinland was developed precisely to overcome these limitations by creating a unified semantic annotation and discovery environment. It originates from the Semantic Computing Research Group at Aalto University, a group that has spent years developing semantic technologies, ontologies, and linked data infrastructures. Their goal with DataFinland was to build a platform capable of enhancing dataset descriptions using semantic standards, so that data becomes easier to find not only for human users but also for automated systems that depend on machine-understandable structures. At the core of this initiative lies the understanding that open data has value only when it is accessible, comprehensible, and interoperable, and metadata plays the essential role in achieving all three. The platform allows datasets to be annotated with richer metadata through an online environment, ensuring that the descriptions follow controlled vocabularies and ontological structures. This solves one of the most persistent issues in open-data ecosystems: the heterogeneity of dataset descriptions. Without standardized vocabularies, each dataset provider tends to use their own terminology, often inconsistent even within the same organization. DataFinland relies on ontology services, such as ONKI, which provide shared controlled vocabularies that guide users in choosing terms that have consistent definitions and relationships. The tool that enables this annotation process is SAHA, a browser-based editor designed for collaborative semantic markup. Through SAHA, datasets can be described in a structured manner, linking them to appropriate categories, subjects, temporal ranges, geographical areas, and other relevant descriptors. This not only improves the internal consistency of the metadata, but also ensures compatibility with broader semantic-web standards. The metadata model used by DataFinland is built upon a modified version of the voiD vocabulary, which is traditionally employed to describe RDF datasets. However, one of the strengths of this initiative is its ability to extend voiD beyond pure linked data, enabling structured annotation even for datasets that exist in non-RDF formats. This means that CSV files, Excel spreadsheets, or other non-semantic data formats can still be documented semantically, thus bridging the gap between traditional open data repositories and modern linked-data infrastructures. As a result, the annotated metadata becomes a unified layer describing all datasets regardless of their internal format, making cross-dataset search and integration more feasible. Once the annotation phase is completed, DataFinland exposes the enriched metadata through two main channels. First, there is a human-oriented portal equipped with faceted search capabilities. This allows users to filter datasets by themes, organizations, formats, geographic criteria, and more. A faceted search system is particularly valuable because it helps users discover datasets even when they are not sure what exactly they are looking for. It turns the exploration of datasets into an intuitive process where each selected facet narrows the results in a meaningful way. Second, the metadata is also made accessible via a SPARQL endpoint, enabling automated systems, developers, and researchers to perform advanced queries. This dual-access approach illustrates the broader philosophy behind DataFinland: information should be equally readable by humans and machines. By creating this semantically enriched ecosystem, DataFinland demonstrates how national open-data infrastructures can evolve into truly interconnected knowledge bases. The platform acts as a proof of concept showing that semantic technologies can significantly improve the discoverability and reusability of open data. This is especially relevant in governmental contexts where transparency initiatives produce large quantities of data but where the quality of metadata often remains insufficient. The Finnish open data landscape benefits greatly from this approach, as the platform has been applied to datasets coming from various governmental institutions. It effectively transforms fragmented data collections into a coherent and navigable resource. Beyond its technical achievements, DataFinland represents a broader cultural shift toward recognizing metadata as a critical component of digital infrastructure. Without thoughtful metadata, open data can become a collection of isolated files whose content must be manually inspected each time. With semantic annotation, open data instead becomes a knowledge graph, where relationships between datasets are explicit, meaningful, and searchable. This transition not only supports researchers and developers but also aligns with the long-term ambitions of digital governments, which aim to create interoperable data ecosystems that serve society efficiently. Another aspect that makes DataFinland particularly relevant is that it offers an example of how countries can build scalable national data infrastructures without requiring immediate adoption of fully semantic formats across all datasets. Many institutions do not have the resources or expertise to convert their data into RDF or linked-data models. DataFinland provides a softer entry point by allowing them to keep their data in existing formats while still benefiting from a semantic layer added on top. This lowers the barrier to participation and promotes a more inclusive and realistic approach to data modernization. Furthermore, the platform highlights the importance of ontology services and controlled vocabularies in ensuring interoperability. When metadata is attached using terms from shared vocabularies, it becomes easier to link datasets from different domains, identify common themes, and integrate information from multiple providers. This is essential for complex societal questions—such as climate change, healthcare, or transportation—where data typically comes from diverse sources and needs to be combined. DataFinland’s approach enables precisely this kind of integration. In summary, DataFinland can be seen as a model for future open-data ecosystems. It blends semantic technologies with practical tools for annotators, supports both human and machine interaction, and provides a unified metadata layer that enhances the value of datasets without changing their internal structure. By improving discoverability, documentation, and interoperability, it transforms open data from a passive collection of files into an active, searchable, and interconnected resource. Its architecture, combining modified voiD vocabularies, ontology services, and semantically powered indexing, represents an innovative and scalable solution to one of the most persistent problems in open-data infrastructure. The initiative ultimately demonstrates that investing in metadata quality is not just a technical improvement but a strategic step toward a more transparent, efficient, and knowledge-driven digital society.


=== Site 14:  Manufacturing - X Dataspace ===
Chem-X is a groundbreaking initiative aimed at transforming the chemical industry by building a secure, interoperable, and standardized data ecosystem capable of supporting sustainability, competitiveness, and resilience across the chemical value chain. Launched on December 1, 2024, the Chem-X project is publicly funded by the German Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Climate Action (BMWK) and coordinated by BASF, one of the world’s largest chemical companies. The central ambition of Chem-X is to enable seamless data exchange about material properties, sustainability metrics, and other key chemical-related information, thereby laying the foundation for Digital Product Passports (DPPs) or Digital Material Passports (DMPs) that can travel along a product’s life cycle, from raw material suppliers through manufacturing to end customers. One of the prime drivers behind Chem-X is the necessity for a digital transformation that supports not just economic efficiency but also environmental and social responsibility. The chemical industry plays a critical role in the global economy. In Germany, for example, it is strongly linked to key sectors such as automotive, construction, electronics, and pharmaceuticals. However, this industry is facing growing pressures: rising energy and raw-material costs, more demanding regulatory requirements, and increasing stakeholder expectations around sustainability. Chem-X responds to these challenges by developing a coherent, open-source data model specific to chemicals. This model is designed to standardize information on sustainability indicators, such as product carbon footprint and recycled content, thus making such data more transparent and usable across the entire supply chain. Structurally, the Chem-X project is organized into a set of work packages to efficiently distribute tasks and ensure progress. According to the project’s documentation, five major work packages (WP) have been defined. These include project management (WP1), the development of digital architecture and data-exchange mechanisms, the design of content standards for material passports, the pilot implementation of data exchange, and verification methods for quality assurance. By dividing the effort in this manner, Chem-X ensures that both the technical design and the strategic aspects of governance, communication, and sustainability alignment are robustly addressed. An essential feature of Chem-X is cooperation among a broad consortium of partners, combining the strengths of major chemical companies, technology providers, and standardization players. The partnership includes giants such as BASF, Covestro, Evonik, Henkel, Merck Group, Wacker, Siemens, and Sika. In addition, there are partners who bring specialized expertise: for example, Spherity, which contributes digital identity wallet technology (self-sovereign identity) to ensure secure, trusted identities for materials, products, and stakeholders across the network. Another notable partner is TfS – Together for Sustainability, which develops harmonized metrics and guidelines for sustainability and circularity that will be integrated into the digital product passport concept. The involvement of Catena-X, an ecosystem already active in the automotive industry, is particularly strategic: Catena-X contributes its experience in open, sovereign data exchange to ensure Chem-X builds on proven data-space architectures rather than reinventing from scratch. At the heart of Chem-X’s value proposition is the Digital Material Passport (DMP). This digital document is envisioned to carry critical information about materials — chemical composition, life-cycle sustainability metrics, recyclability, origin, and even safety data — in a machine-readable and interoperable form. By establishing a standard for DMPs, Chem-X hopes to significantly improve transparency along supply chains and enable more circular business models: suppliers can reliably communicate sustainability credentials, customers can make more informed decisions, and regulators can more easily verify compliance. The open-source nature of the underlying data model means that the standard could eventually be adopted widely, minimizing proprietary lock-in and enhancing consistency across the industry. Resilience is another pillar of the Chem-X vision. In volatile markets, chemical companies need to react swiftly to shifts in supply, demand, or regulatory landscapes. The ability to share detailed material data securely through a trusted digital infrastructure means that companies can make more informed decisions, optimize inventories, and better manage risks. Digital material twins — virtual representations of real materials enriched with real-time data — will support scenario planning and predictive analysis, helping firms anticipate disruptions or tailor production more dynamically. Sustainability in Chem-X is not just about environmental metrics; it's also about creating a circular economy. By enabling better data exchange on recycled content, chemical origin, and end-of-life attributes, the project fosters reuse and valorization of materials. Suppliers who produce more sustainable chemicals can share their credentials, and downstream users can build on that to close material loops. Moreover, transparency about carbon footprints can drive competition: companies that optimize for low emissions could be rewarded in the market, while others are pushed to improve. This is aligned with emerging regulatory demands for digital product transparency and traceability. Implementing such an ambitious data ecosystem also requires strong governance, technical standards, and trust frameworks. Chem-X addresses this by defining clear protocols for data exchange, authentication, and verification. The work package dedicated to verification ensures that the data in DMPs is reliable and that stakeholders can trust the information. They plan to develop methods to audit and validate data, as well as to ensure provenance and quality. This is critical: without trust in data, the entire digital passport concept would fail. Moreover, identity management is tackled through Spherity’s self-sovereign identity solutions, which allow materials, products, and companies to be uniquely identified while preserving data sovereignty. Technically, Chem-X fosters interoperability through the development of a standardized data model that is open-source and chemistry-specific. This data model becomes the backbone for DMPs, enabling various actors to use a common language when representing chemical attributes. The digital architecture that supports data exchange is designed to be modular and scalable — it must accommodate large volumes of material data, different data formats, and different levels of maturity among participating companies. The modularity also means that future extensions are possible: Chem-X can evolve to cover more use cases, incorporate additional metrics, or support more participants without redesigning the whole system. To ensure that the concepts are not purely theoretical, Chem-X foresees demonstration and pilot implementations. Work packages are dedicated to building real use cases and pilot scenarios, where companies can actually generate, exchange, and verify digital material passports. These pilots will test both the technical feasibility and the business value of the system: can data be shared reliably? Can sustainability metrics be calculated and trusted? Can downstream actors use the DMPs to make better decisions? The results of these pilots will be critical, because they will guide how Chem-X can be scaled, refined, and potentially adopted more broadly by the chemical industry. The expected impact of Chem-X is multifaceted. Firstly, by standardizing chemical data and enabling transparent information flow, the project has the potential to greatly improve sustainability across the industry: circular material flows, lower carbon footprints, and traceability. Secondly, by making data exchange more efficient and interoperable, it can reduce costs and friction in the supply chain, improving competitiveness. Thirdly, resilience will be strengthened: companies will be better equipped to adapt to market shocks and regulatory change. Finally, the digital infrastructure and governance principles developed in Chem-X could serve as a blueprint for other “X-industries” — other sectors that rely on materials and complex supply chains, such as manufacturing, automotive, or electronics. Of course, alongside the potential benefits, Chem-X faces major challenges. Building a data ecosystem across a highly fragmented and traditionally secretive industry like chemicals is not trivial. Companies must be persuaded to share data that they may have guarded jealously, especially if it involves proprietary formulations or cost-sensitive processes. Data privacy, intellectual property, and competitive risk must be managed carefully. Standardizing data models in a domain as complex as chemistry is itself a huge task: agreeing on what sustainability metrics matter, how to calculate them, and how to verify them requires deep technical and domain expertise. Moreover, adoption risk is non-trivial. To succeed at scale, Chem-X must attract a broad set of participants: major chemical producers, downstream users, regulators, and technology providers. Without enough critical mass, the ecosystem may fragment or fail to deliver real value. Technical adoption may also be slow: not all companies will be ready to invest in systems to produce or consume digital material passports. Regulatory alignment is also a factor: the value of DMPs becomes much higher if they align with existing or upcoming regulations (for example, for ecodesign or digital product compliance), but legal frameworks vary by country and region. Despite these challenges, Chem-X is uniquely well-positioned to succeed. Its consortium brings together leading chemical companies (like BASF, Covestro, Evonik, Henkel, Merck, Wacker, Sika), major technology firms (SAP, Siemens), and specialized players (Spherity, Catena-X, TfS) who together cover technological, market, and governance dimensions. This alignment of interests is critical: it means that technical standards, data models, and pilot scenarios are not being designed in isolation but with input from the most relevant stakeholders in the chemical value chain. In addition, the timing of Chem-X is very favorable. As environmental regulations tighten and circular economy principles become more central to industrial strategy, there is a growing demand for transparency and digital traceability in material production. The concept of a digital product or material passport is gaining traction globally, not only as a tool for sustainability reporting but also as a mechanism to underpin regulatory compliance and industry certification. Chem-X’s open-source data model and interoperability-first architecture could become a reference point for future policymaking and industry standardization. In conclusion, Chem-X represents a bold and transformative effort to modernize the chemical industry through data. It is not just about digitizing information — it is about rethinking how material-related data is structured, shared, verified, and used throughout the value chain. By enabling Digital Material Passports based on open, interoperable standards, Chem-X promises to deliver gains in sustainability, competitiveness, and resilience. Its success could mark a turning point in how chemical companies collaborate, how products are designed and circulated, and how industries transition toward more circular and transparent business models. If fully realized and widely adopted, Chem-X could become more than a project: it could become the backbone of a new, digital, sustainable chemical economy.


=== Site 15: Public Procurement Data Space (PPDS) ===
The Public Procurement Data Space (PPDS) is a major European initiative created to transform how procurement data is collected, shared, analysed, and reused across the EU. Building on a procurement landscape in which more than 250,000 public authorities spend around €2 trillion every year—roughly 13–15% of EU GDP—the PPDS responds to a long-standing structural problem: although procurement generates vast amounts of data, only a small share has been accessible in a harmonised, machine-readable format suitable for modern analytics. About 20% of higher-value procedures are published through centralised EU systems such as TED, while the remaining 80% of procedures below EU thresholds are dispersed across national or regional portals, stored in different systems, in different formats, and often without structured fields or unique identifiers. This fragmentation has made reuse difficult, limiting transparency, policy evaluation, SME participation, and value for money in public spending. The PPDS is designed to close this gap by creating a unified, interoperable, and trustworthy European data infrastructure capable of supporting near real-time monitoring and advanced analytics. It leverages new technologies introduced through eForms—mandatory for above-threshold procedures from late 2023 onward—which allow procurement data to be collected in a structured way for the first time, including information relevant to green procurement, social considerations, innovation, the use of EU funds, and compliance with regulations. Launched publicly in 2024 and progressively implemented, the PPDS is expected to reach full operational capacity by 2025, when participating Member States will have connected their national portals, integrated historical data, and completed the core analytics toolkit. The PPDS is organised around four technical layers. The Data Sources Layer connects the federated network of procurement data providers, including TED, national publication portals, and potentially other systems such as business registers, beneficial ownership databases, and e-invoicing platforms. This preserves decentralised control—Member States decide which data beyond mandatory EU notices they share—while enabling unified access. The Integration Layer harmonises all incoming data and transforms it into a common semantic structure using the eProcurement ontology. It validates completeness, checks data quality, reconciles identifiers, and resolves duplication, ensuring datasets can be safely used for cross-country analytics. The Analytics Layer provides tools for data exploration, querying, and advanced AI-driven analysis, enabling activities such as detecting procurement trends, monitoring competition levels, building predictive models of supply and demand, analysing buyer behaviour over time, identifying risky patterns indicative of fraud or collusion, and supporting early-warning or strategic forecasting functions. The Client Layer is the user-facing environment, giving policymakers, public buyers, SMEs, journalists, researchers, auditors, and civil society organisations access to the integrated dataset and analytics in a tailored interface. Its aim is to make complex procurement information understandable and usable for audiences with different levels of technical knowledge, while respecting confidentiality and differentiated access rights. The benefits of the PPDS are significant for all stakeholder groups. For companies—particularly SMEs—it levels the playing field by offering a one-stop entry point to thousands of tenders across Europe, with higher data quality and earlier visibility into opportunities. SMEs gain the ability to analyse buyer profiles, identify markets where their capabilities match demand, and monitor long-term pipelines, which supports more strategic and less resource-intensive bidding. For public buyers, the PPDS brings the possibility of data-driven planning, better forecasting, and benchmarking against peers. They can more easily identify opportunities for joint procurement, assess the performance of suppliers, monitor participation by SMEs, detect contracts with only one bid, and evaluate whether procurement choices advance policy goals such as sustainability or social inclusion. Integrated analytics also support fraud and collusion detection by identifying unusual patterns that would be nearly impossible to detect manually. At the same time, automated data collection reduces reporting burdens, especially for obligations related to green procurement, innovation, social considerations, and the use of EU funds such as the RRF. For policymakers, the PPDS offers unprecedented visibility into public spending across the EU. It enables near real-time monitoring of procurement flows, improved oversight of supply chains, crisis management capability, evidence-based policy adjustments, and more accurate assessment of how procurement contributes to objectives such as the Green Deal or industrial resilience. During crises—such as pandemics, energy disruptions or geopolitical shocks—better data can support quicker reactions by revealing shortages, price changes, and vulnerability in essential supply lines. For the public, researchers, and journalists, PPDS increases transparency and accountability. Access to high-quality, harmonised procurement data enables independent scrutiny, facilitates investigations, supports academic research, and encourages innovative analytics that can feed back into the platform over time. Participation by Member States is voluntary but strongly encouraged. To join, Member States must ensure high data quality at source, implement machine-readable interfaces, standardise the use of unique identifiers, adopt eForms also for below-threshold procedures where possible, and gradually extend data capture to cover the entire procurement cycle including post-award completion notices. While Member States fund the modernisation of their own national systems, substantial EU support is available through the Digital Europe Programme, the RRF, the Technical Support Instrument, and structural funds. Access to the PPDS for users is planned to be free of charge. From a governance perspective, the PPDS emphasises transparency, quality assurance, and continuous improvement. A data-quality dashboard monitors completeness and validity, while feedback loops and change requests allow Member States and user communities to contribute to ongoing enhancements. The official launch in 2024 introduced the platform publicly, including demonstrations, technical discussions on semantics and quality rules, and exchanges between EU institutions, Member States, businesses, and public buyers on how the PPDS can transform procurement practices. Overall, the Public Procurement Data Space is central to the EU’s broader vision of a modern, data-driven, transparent, and efficient procurement ecosystem. By addressing fragmentation, improving interoperability, and enabling systematic and advanced analysis, the PPDS strengthens the integrity and performance of public spending, empowers SMEs with better access to markets, supports oversight and crisis resilience, and aligns procurement with major policy objectives such as sustainability, digitalisation, and innovation.


=== Site 16: The Once-Only Technical System (OOTS) ===
The Once‑Only Technical System, often abbreviated as OOTS, stands as a central pillar in the European Commission’s broader vision of building a truly interoperable and citizen‑centric digital single market. At its heart, OOTS is designed to significantly reduce bureaucratic burden by allowing public administrations across EU Member States to exchange official data and evidence — but only when a citizen or business explicitly requests it. Under this framework, individuals no longer need to manually provide the same document multiple times to different public authorities: once a piece of data is held by a competent authority, OOTS makes it possible for other authorities to retrieve it in a secure, standardized, and trusted manner. This concept is often known as the “once‑only” principle, and it hinges on strengthening trust, preserving user consent, and making cross-border administrative processes smoother, more efficient, and more reliable. Technically, OOTS functions as a distributed data space rooted in Europe's Digital Building Blocks. Rather than centralizing all information in a single database, it connects national systems via a network of interoperable components, ensuring that data stays in its most authentic source yet becomes accessible upon request. This distributed architecture allows Member States to maintain control over their systems while still participating in a shared infrastructure. Central to its design are EU‑wide building blocks like electronic identification (eID), secure document exchange (eDelivery), and electronic signature (eSignature). These components provide the trust and security layer necessary for sensitive data exchange across borders, ensuring that only authorized exchanges happen and that personal data protection standards are respected. From a legal and regulatory standpoint, OOTS is enshrined in the Single Digital Gateway Regulation, which provides the foundation for cross-border administrative services. By leveraging that legal basis, OOTS ensures that public administrations operate under a clear, regulated framework. The technical specifications of OOTS, defined in implementing regulation, outline how data should be requested, which services should be used, and how public administrations must respond. This regulation is crucial because it not only defines the interoperability standards but also ensures that the system respects the rights of citizens, including the right to control when and what data is shared. OOTS also features common services for public administrations. These services include an “Evidence Broker” which helps identify what evidence types (documents or data) a user can request, a “Data Service Directory” that provides information about which authorities hold which data, and a “Semantic Repository” that ensures the requested evidence is described according to shared data models, metadata schemata, and data formats. This semantic alignment is critical for correctly interpreting data across different jurisdictions and administrative domains. For citizens and businesses, OOTS promises multiple concrete benefits. It dramatically reduces the administrative effort needed for cross-border procedures: for instance, when someone moves to another EU country, starts a business abroad, or needs to handle official paperwork in a different Member State, they will no longer need to manually collect, scan and upload documents like birth certificates, diplomas, or proof of residence. Instead, they can request that relevant public institutions themselves retrieve these documents on their behalf, provided the user grants explicit permission. This not only saves time and effort but also reduces the risk of errors and potential fraud associated with document handling. Moreover, because the data is retrieved directly from the authoritative source, the information received by public administrations is always the latest version — reducing the risk of outdated or falsified evidence. On the governance side, OOTS is managed in a way that balances innovation, trust, and user empowerment. There is a dedicated service desk that supports users — public authorities, Member States, or technical implementers — in handling technical requests, incidents, or use-case questions. This service desk acts as a first point of contact, escalating complex issues to a project management office when needed. In addition, there are clearly defined service-level agreements (SLAs) for common services such as user management, which assure participants of operational reliability. A crucial aspect of OOTS’s trustworthiness comes from its strict alignment with data protection regulations. The system is designed to allow data exchange only when explicitly requested by the end user, and to support previews of the data before sharing. Public authorities cannot simply pull personal data; instead, they make a request, the user is informed, and only then is the data exchanged, always maintaining transparency. On a broader scale, OOTS is not just a technical infrastructure: it is a foundational data space for public administrations. It sets a blueprint for future data spaces by showing how cross-border, cross-sector data sharing can be done in compliance with European values like privacy, transparency, and interoperability. The Once‑Only Technical System thus represents more than a convenience for citizens: it is a building block for a modern, digital, and resilient European public administration ecosystem. By enabling efficient, consent-based data reuse, OOTS can spark a significant transformation in how governments operate, reducing redundant processes, cutting administrative costs, and enhancing user experience in public services. The implementation of OOTS also highlights important challenges and opportunities. From a technical perspective, integrating dozens of national digital systems — each with its own legacy, legal context, and data standards — is complex. Harmonizing data formats, semantic models, and authentication protocols across Member States demands careful coordination. The onboarding of authorities is a major task: tens of thousands of competent public bodies will need to be connected to the system, adopt the common services, and ensure compliance with the semantic repository. At the same time, the ambition behind OOTS opens the door to significant innovation: by standardizing how public administrations share data, the system can support more advanced cross-border services in the future, such as automated verification for social benefits, streamlined mobility procedures, or more dynamic public sector digital services. It may also nurture trust among citizens and businesses, as they begin to experience the tangible benefits of data reuse while maintaining control over their information. In terms of long-term impact, OOTS has the potential to catalyze a shift in the culture of public service design. When governments embrace a once-only data paradigm, they can reduce friction, increase trust, and make cross-border public services truly seamless. The benefits ripple outward: less duplication in administrative work, more efficient use of taxpayer money, and more engaged citizens. The system also supports EU strategic goals such as the Digital Decade’s objectives, by contributing to a data-driven public sector and fostering interoperable governance. Overall, the OOTSHUB / Once‑Only Technical System stands as a cornerstone of Europe’s digital transformation. It marries a high-level vision of a digital single market with user-centric design, legal certainty, and technical robustness. While challenges remain, if fully realized, OOTS can become a powerful enabler of cross-border cooperation, administrative simplification, and trustworthy digital government, laying the groundwork for a future where public administrations across Europe truly talk to each other in the interest of their citizens.
